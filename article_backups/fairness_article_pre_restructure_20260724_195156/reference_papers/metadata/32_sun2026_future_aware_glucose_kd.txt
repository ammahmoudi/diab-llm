Future-aware blood glucose
forecasting using knowledge
distillation with transformer-based
sequence-to-sequence models
Xiaoyu Sun, Hongru Li & Xia Yu
Accurate blood glucose level (BGL) forecasting is critical for diabetes self-management and clinical
decision-making. Although deep learning models based on continuous glucose monitoring (CGM) data
have achieved encouraging results, most approaches rely exclusively on historical observations and
cannot explicitly account for future disturbances, such as insulin delivery and meal intake, that are
unavailable at deployment. To address this limitation, we propose a future-aware learning framework
for multi-step BGL prediction that leverages privileged information during training while preserving
deployability at inference. A Transformer-based teacher model is trained offline using both historical
CGM data and future disturbance information to learn disturbance-aware temporal representations.
A student model with a similar sequence-to-sequence structure is then trained using knowledge
distillation to approximate the teacher’s representations based solely on historical inputs, enabling
real-time forecasting without access to future data. The proposed framework is evaluated on the
publicly available OhioT1DM and AZT1D datasets for prediction horizons ranging from 30 to 120
minutes and compared with several established methods. The results show consistent reductions
in root mean squared error and mean absolute error, together with improved clinical reliability as
assessed by Clarke error grid analysis, with over 90% of predictions falling within clinically acceptable
regions. These findings demonstrate the potential of future-aware training strategies to enhance
glucose forecasting performance under realistic deployment constraints.
Diabetes mellitus is a chronic metabolic condition that affects around 589 million people worldwide and is
characterized by sustained hyperglycemia due to the body’s inability to produce or effectively utilize insulin 1.
Type 1 diabetes mellitus (T1DM), in particular, results from autoimmune destruction of insulin-producing β
-cells in the pancreas, necessitating lifelong exogenous insulin therapy. Maintaining blood glucose levels (BGLs)
within a narrow euglycemic range (typically 70-180 mg/dL) is essential to prevent both acute complications
(e.g., hypoglycemia, diabetic ketoacidosis) and long-term comorbidities (e.g., retinopathy, neuropathy, and
cardiovascular disease) 2,3. However, achieving optimal glycemic control remains a significant challenge,
especially due to the nonlinear and delayed physiological responses to insulin, meals, stress, physical activity,
and other lifestyle factors4.
In clinical practice, individuals with T1DM rely on continuous glucose monitoring (CGM) systems and
insulin pumps or multiple daily injections to regulate BGLs. However, a critical limitation lies in the inherent lags
of glucose sensing and insulin kinetics. Subcutaneously infused insulin exhibits a delayed onset and prolonged
action compared to endogenous insulin. Besides, CGM systems measure interstitial glucose rather than plasma
glucose levels, which takes time for glucose to be transported between plasma and interstitial fluid 4–6. But it is
worth noting that the CGM readings are referred to as BGL in this work. These temporal mismatches make
real-time glycemic regulation challenging and often lead to overcorrection or underestimation of insulin needs,
resulting in frequent episodes of hypo- or hyperglycemia. T o mitigate these risks, accurate BGL forecasting has
been widely recognized as a key component in decision support systems for diabetes management.
T o estimate future BGLs, a broad range of data-driven prediction models has been developed based on
CGM data and related physiological signals. Traditional approaches, including autoregressive models, Kalman
filters, Gaussian process regression, and support vector machines7–12, have been widely investigated in modeling
glucose dynamics. While these models offer some interpretability and robustness, they often fall short in
capturing the nonlinear and multiscale dynamics in glycemic metabolism. More recently, the surge in deep

mechanisms, transformer architectures, reinforcement learning, variational autoencoders, etc.20–25.
Among these advanced techniques, the encoder-decoder framework has gained particular attention for
sequence-to-sequence (seq2seq) forecasting tasks, including glucose prediction. This architecture consists of
two components: an encoder that transforms the input sequence into a latent representation, and a decoder that
generates the output sequence based on this representation. The use of attention mechanisms in the encoder-
decoder framework has further improved performance by allowing the model to focus on the most relevant
time steps in the input sequence. Transformers, originally introduced for natural language processing tasks, have
recently been adapted to BGL forecasting and have shown state-of-the-art performance21,26–28. However, despite
these advancements, a key limitation of the end-to-end deep learning models for BGL forecasting is that they
rely solely on past observations and assume the future is independent of external disturbances. In real-world
scenarios, unobserved future events, such as unannounced meals, unrecorded physical activity, stress, or insulin
infusion, can dramatically alter glycemic trends. These future disturbances are not available at prediction time
and are thus omitted from the model input, leading to systematic prediction errors, particularly during periods
of rapid glucose change. Although various attempts have been made to compensate for this uncertainty using
data augmentation, probabilistic modeling, or ensemble methods, the challenge of representing unseen future
influences remains a fundamental bottleneck.
To address these limitations, we proposed a knowledge-distillation-based approach that improved the
prediction accuracy of future BGLs by learning from future disturbances. The main contributions of this study
are as follows:
 (1) We develop a future-aware training framework for multi-step blood glucose level prediction that leverages
privileged future disturbance information during training while maintaining deployability using historical
data only at inference time.
 (2) We design a Transformer-based teacher student architecture in which a teacher model learns distur -
bance-aware temporal representations from complete sequences containing both past and future glu -
cose-related signals, and a student model approximates these representations using historical observations
alone through knowledge distillation.
 (3) We introduce a shared decoder strategy that enables consistent decoding of teacher and student latent rep-
resentations into future blood glucose trajectories, facilitating effective comparison and transfer of predic-
tive information.
 (4) We conduct a comprehensive evaluation on a publicly available clinical continuous glucose monitoring
dataset across multiple prediction horizons, demonstrating consistent performance improvements over
baseline deep learning approaches and clinically acceptable prediction accuracy as assessed by Clarke error
grid analysis.
In summary, this study presents a future-aware learning framework for multi-step blood glucose prediction
that addresses the mismatch between training assumptions and deployment constraints in real-world diabetes
management. By exploiting future disturbance information during offline training through a knowledge-
distillation-based teacher student architecture, the proposed approach enables a deployable student model
to capture future-relevant temporal representations using historical data only. Comprehensive evaluations
on clinical CGM data demonstrate that the framework provides consistent improvements over baseline deep
learning models across multiple prediction horizons while maintaining clinical reliability. These findings
highlight both the potential and the practical limits of transferring future-aware information to history-only
predictors, offering insights for the development of robust and deployable glucose forecasting systems.
Related works
The recent rapid development of wearable sensing and deep learning techniques provides novel opportunities
for modeling the glucose metabolism of individuals with T1DM accurately, which is critical for diabetic
management. The conventional DNN structures, including FNN, RNN, and CNN, have been widely investigated
in modeling glucose metabolism and forecasting future BGLs.
Rabby et al. 29 combined the stacked LSTM model with Kalman filter, where the Kalman filter was applied
to CGM readings for improving the robustness of the DNN model to sensor noise and errors. Wang et al. 30
proposed to reduce the non-stationarity of glycemic dynamics by decomposing CGM data with variational
modal decomposition method and forecasting future BGLs with an LSTM model. Shuvo et al. 31 incorporated
the stacked LSTM model with the multi-task learning framework to prevent overfitting. Considering that the
personalized data is limited, Shen et al. 32 investigated the amount of data required for a stacked LSTM model
and proposed an incrementally retrained LSTM model for BGL forecasting, where the model was finetuned as
the distribution of new data shifted. Li et al. 33 and Bhargav et al. 34 investigated the performance of temporal
convolutional network (TCN), which is designed for capturing temporal features in time series with a lighter
computational burden, in forecasting future BGLs. Talebi et al.35 extended the TCN-based BGL prediction model
by integrating an attention mechanism, which improved the model’s ability in capturing both local and global
temporal correlations in glycemic metabolism. Li et al.19 and Jaloli et al.36 proposed to forecast future BGLs with

a hybrid CNN and LSTM structure where the CNN layers were utilized to capture local patterns in the glycemic
metabolism and LSTM was adopted to model the temporal dependencies. Sun et al.37 and Xie et al.38 investigated
the efficiency of LSTM model, Bidirectional LSTM (BiLSTM), and TCN in short-term BGL prediction and
found the RNN model had higher prediction accuracy compared to conventional machine learning models.
Bhimireddy et al. 39 compared the performance of conventional BGL prediction models based on LSTM,
BiLSTM, and CRNN with sequence-to-sequence (Seq2seq) models and found the Seq2seq models outperform
the conventional DNN models. The Seq2seq models generally adopt an encoder-decoder structure where the
encoder extracts complex features from input sequences and the decoder recovers future BGL sequences from
the obtained deep features. As the future BGLs are predicted simultaneously, the Seq2seq models are more
robust compared to conventional BGL forecast models.
Motivated by the representation ability of encoder-decoder structures, Xu et al.40 proposed a glucose prediction
model based on the encoder-decoder structure, where the LSTM was utilized to encode the input information
and an attention-based decoder was designed to predict future BGLs. To address the challenge of limited data
resources, He et al.41 developed a personalized autocorrelation encoder that mapped diverse physiological factors
into a unified latent space to obtain sparse representations. Considering the potential influence of instability in
glucose dynamics, Xue et al. 42 developed a BGL forecasting model based on Informer, where the periodic and
trend features were extracted to enhance the prediction capacity of the model. Lee et al. 26 incorporated the
Transformer encoder with a multi-task learning framework where temporal correlations in glycemic dynamics
were captured by the Transformer encoder and further utilized for BGL prediction and abnormal glycemic
events classification. Zhu et al.27 proposed a light-weight BGL prediction model by integrating LSTM encoder
with self-attention mechanism, and the population model enabled a prolonged period on wearable devices. Bian
et al.43 proposed a hybrid BGL prediction model with two encoders, where a stacked LSTM-based encoder was
developed to obtain temporal features and a Transformer-based encoder was utilized to enhance long-term
dependency. Karagoz et al. 44 conducted a study to investigate the performance of diverse Transformer-based
models for BGL forecast against different lengths of history information and found that the best prediction
performance was achieved with an input length of one week. Despite the extremely long history of information
being utilized for predicting future BGLs, the potential disturbances within the prediction horizon are ignored,
which may harm the long-term BGL forecast.
Methods
This work aims to develop a BGL prediction model where the potential disturbances can be encoded into the
latent space and transferred through knowledge distillation for improving the model’s capacity. Particularly, a
Transformer encoder-based teacher model was designed to mine the temporal correlations from the historical
observations, together with the future disturbances. The obtained features were transposed to a shared decoder
to ensure the latent representations were related to future BGLs. Then, the student model was developed through
knowledge distillation to mimic the behaviors of the teacher model based solely on historical observations,
therefore, incorporating information of future disturbances into the BGL forecasting model. The overall
framework of the proposed BGL prediction model is depicted in Fig. 1.
Feature extraction
The variation of BGL is influenced by many factors, including meal intake, insulin infusion, physical activity,
stress, etc. Among those factors, meal intake is the major source of glucose, and BGL usually increases rapidly
after carbohydrate consumption. Differently, as the only hormone that promotes the transport, utilization,
and storage of plasma glucose, BGL generally decreases after insulin infusion. To establish an accurate BGL
forecasting model, the influence of those factors should be taken into account. However, the meal information is
usually self-recorded by individuals with diabetes as a sparse impulse signal that happens 3-5 times per day. And

Detailed model structures
Teacher encoder
For medium- and long-term glucose prediction, potential disturbances such as carbohydrate intake and insulin
administration exert a substantial influence on future blood glucose dynamics. To explicitly leverage the effects
of such disturbances during training, a teacher encoder is designed to extract temporal representations from
both historical observations xpast and future disturbance information xf uture. The overall structure of the
teacher model is illustrated in Fig. 2(a), where Transformer-based encoders are employed to capture long-range
temporal dependencies.
The Transformer is a sequence-to-sequence architecture originally developed for natural language
processing47. It consists of an encoder that learns contextualized representations through self-attention and
feed-forward layers, and a decoder that generates outputs by selectively attending to relevant encoded features
via cross-attention. Owing to its ability to retain global temporal context and identify informative time steps, the
Transformer has demonstrated superior performance over recurrent neural networks for long sequential data.
Consistent with prior studies in glucose prediction26, only the Transformer encoder is utilized in this work. Each
encoder comprises an embedding layer, positional encoding, and stacked multi-head attention blocks, followed
by normalization and residual connections.
T o obtain comprehensive temporal representations from both past observations and future disturbances, two
separate Transformer encoders are adopted. Each encoder applies multi-head self-attention to extract high-level
temporal features, where the self-attention operation is defined as47:

Beyond independent encoding, the teacher model further employs a cross-attention mechanism to
fuse information from historical observations and future disturbances. Specifically, the encoded historical
representations are used as queries, while the encoded future disturbance representations serve as keys and
values, allowing the model to selectively attend to future disturbance patterns that are most relevant to the
historical glucose context. This cross-attention operation enables the teacher encoder to learn disturbance-aware
temporal representations by explicitly modeling the interaction between past physiological states and future
exogenous inputs. The fused representations are then aggregated and passed to a shared decoder to generate
future blood glucose predictions.
Student encoder
For real-world prediction, the model has access only to historical observations. The student encoder therefore
mirrors the teacher encoder but processes only xpast (Fig. 2 (b)). It uses the same embedding and sinusoidal
positional encoding layers as the teacher to preserve temporal ordering. The embedded inputs pass through
identical stacks of multi-head attention and feed-forward blocks with residual connections, producing a
historical latent representation.

To ensure the student learns rich temporal features despite missing future information, an additional
MLP projection head with residual connections refines its output. This projection head aligns the student’s
representation space with that of the teacher during knowledge distillation, narrowing the gap between
disturbance-aware and historical-only features. In effect, the student encoder learns to approximate the teacher’s
latent space and capture implicit disturbance effects without direct access to future inputs.
Shared decoder
A single shared decoder (Fig.  2c) maps the latent representations from both the teacher and student encoders
to future BGL predictions. The decoder consists of stacked fully connected layers with nonlinear activation
(ReLU), dropout for regularization, and a final linear output layer. The shared model ensures that both teacher
and student benefit from the same decoding process, improving consistency between training and inference.
Knowledge distillation process
In practical deployment scenarios, future disturbance information such as meal intake and insulin administration
is unavailable at inference time, even though it can be observed during offline training. This mismatch between
training and deployment conditions limits the performance of conventional blood glucose forecasting models
that rely solely on historical observations. To address this issue, we employ a knowledge distillation strategy
that enables a deployable student model to internalize information learned under relaxed training assumptions.
The teacher model is trained offline using both historical CGM measurements and future disturbance
inputs. By observing future meals and insulin events, the teacher encoder learns disturbance-aware temporal
representations that capture how exogenous factors shape subsequent glucose dynamics. These representations
encode not only glucose trends but also the anticipated direction, magnitude, and temporal persistence of
disturbance-induced effects.

deployment constraints, using only historical CGM data as input. Since future disturbance information is

Results
Dataset and preprocess
OhioT1DM dataset
The OhioT1DM dataset 48 contains eight weeks of continuous data from 12 individuals living with T1DM (5
female, 7 male; aged 20 60 years) under free-living conditions. The OhioT1DM dataset was naturally separated
into training and testing datasets. The training dataset was utilized for model development and validation, and
the testing dataset was adopted for testing the performance of the proposed model. All participants used insulin
pump therapy (Medtronic 530G or 630G) and wore Medtronic Enlite CGM sensors, which recorded BGL every
5 minutes. In addition to CGM readings and insulin information, the dataset includes physiological data from
fitness bands, such as heart rate, galvanic skin response (GSR), skin temperature, and accelerometry, as well as
self-reported events, including meal intake, sleep, work, stress, hypoglycemia, illness, and exercise. The dataset
was originally divided into training and testing sets as summarized in Table 1. Basal, bolus, and self-reported
meal information were aligned with CGM records at a uniform 5-minute sampling interval.
Because of sensor replacement and communication issues, missing CGM segments occur in both the training
and testing sets. In the training data, gaps shorter than 30 minutes were linearly interpolated from surrounding
values, and longer gaps were discarded to avoid introducing additional noise. In the test data, the last available
CGM value was carried forward until a new measurement became available, ensuring no leakage of future
information into the model during evaluation. This pre-processing strategy maintains temporal consistency
while minimizing bias from missing data, as done in most related works.
AZT1D dataset
The AZT1D dataset49 contains data collected from 25 subjects with T1DM who regulated their BGLs with the
automated insulin delivery system. Most individuals provide information, including CGM readings (Dexcom
G6 Pro) with a sampling time of 5 minutes, insulin administration, and carbohydrate intake, that lasts for over
6-8 weeks, reflecting typical outpatient care practices. In order to evaluate the performance of the proposed
method, the first 80% data were utilized for model development and the remaining 20% were keep for testing.
Similar to the OhioT1DM dataset, there are potential missing values in the CGM readings. In this work,
constant CGM readings lasting over 30 minutes were treated as missing values. For the training dataset, the gaps

Experiment setting
All methods were implemented in PyTorch and executed on a single NVIDIA RTX 5090 D GPU with CUDA
version 12.9. The proposed BGL prediction model was compared with several state-of-the-art approaches,
including an LSTM model 39, a BiLSTM model 39, a CRNN model 19, a Transformer model 26, and a zero-hold
baseline where the most recent CGM value was used as the forecast. In addition, we examined the performance
of (i) the traditional Seq2One regression modeling strategy, which predicts a single future target value from
historical observations, and (ii) the Seq2seq modeling strategy, which forecasts the entire sequence of future
targets simultaneously.
For both the teacher and student encoders, input sequences were embedded into a 64-dimensional latent
space. The number of multi-head attention units was set to 4. Each encoder block contained two 1D CNN
layers, where features were firstly projected to 128 dimensions and then mapped back to 64. The number of
encoder blocks was fixed at N1 = N2 = N3 =3 , determined through cross-validation on the training set. The
hyperparameters α and β are jointly searched within [0.8, 0.5, 0.2, 0.1] and [0.5, 0.2, 0.1, 0.05, 0.01]. The shared
decoder followed the same structure as in the CRNN model19.
The glucose values reported by CGM, estimated IOB and COB within the 3-hour historical window were
used to predict future BGL values up to 2 hours as a simultaneous sequence. The input and output of the models
are normalized through min-max scaling. Models were trained with a batch size of 512, a learning rate of 0.0001
using the Adam optimizer, and an early stopping criterion of no improvement in validation loss for 20 epochs
or reaching the maximum training steps 200. All models were initially trained on the population data and
subsequently fine-tuned on individual data to enable personalized BGL prediction.

Discussion
Seq2One and Seq2Seq are the two major modeling strategies in blood glucose forecasting, where target values are
predicted separately or simultaneously. Among the Seq2One models investigated in this study, the Transformer-
based model achieved the lowest prediction error (RMSE and MAE) for short- to long-term predictions, while
the CRNN performed worst in short-term prediction. This may be because local features were pooled in CRNN,
losing patterns critical for short-term forecasts. Similarly, bidirectional information in the BiLSTM did not
improve medium- to long-term prediction, as reflected in its larger RMSEs and MAEs. By contrast, Seq2Seq
models generally improved prediction accuracy, as simultaneous prediction of future BGLs forces the models to
generalize over the entire sequence. However, we also observed some decline in short-term forecast ability with
RNN-based Seq2Seq models despite their overall advantage in long-term predictions.
From a clinical perspective, the infusion of subcutaneous insulin and the consumption of meals are among
the strongest drivers of glucose excursions. Forecasting BGL without explicitly modeling these disturbances
raises the risk of under- or over-estimating future glucose levels, which can lead to missed opportunities for
corrective action or unnecessary interventions. By incorporating future disturbance information into the
modeling process, our Transformer-based Seq2Seq model achieved significant improvements in short- to long-
term predictions (RMSE improved by 0.65-4.33 mg/dL and MAE improved by 0.37-3.25 mg/dL with OhioT1DM
dataset, RMSE improved by 2.11-7.64 mg/dL and MAE improved by 1.48-6.58 mg/dL with AZT1D dataset).
The results indicate that disturbance-aware modeling particularly benefits medium- to long-term forecasts,
even though future insulin and meal information is not inherently available in the historical data. Clinically,
more accurate medium- to long-term forecasts can enable better timing of insulin boluses, meal planning, and
proactive prevention of hypo- or hyperglycemia.
When the knowledge obtained by the teacher encoder was transferred to the student model, the student,
who lacks direct future disturbance inputs, was able to approximate disturbance-aware patterns and improve
BGL forecasts. This demonstrates the practical deployability of our framework, that is, the student model can be
implemented in real-world systems (e.g., decision-support or closed-loop insulin delivery systems) where only
historical data is available, yet still benefits from knowledge distilled from richer datasets during training. As
expected, for short-term prediction, the variables that may influence the changes of future BGLs have already
been included in the model input, the improvement of model performance is not significant. However, as
the prediction horizon becomes larger, a considerable difference can be observed in the prediction metrics,
demonstrating the importance of incorporating future disturbance for long-term glucose forecasting.
Figures  4 and 5 compared the predicted and measured BGL trajectories for 30- and 60-minute-ahead
forecasts in subjects with the least and most missing gaps. For 30-minute-ahead prediction, the model closely
tracked CGM measurements when sensor data was available. However, larger prediction errors emerged during
missing CGM segments, which is expected given that zero-hold extrapolation is a weak imputation strategy. We
also observed that prediction errors were larger when CGM trajectories saturated, and that sensor noise became
more influential at longer horizons, even though predicted values continued to follow the measurements overall.

Despite the advantages of our proposed knowledge-distillation-based model, several limitations remain. The
proposed model assumes that the individuals follow a regular daily routine. Even though it is the underlying
assumption for predictive tasks, the behavior of the individual may change considerably. To mitigate the influence
of abnormal patterns, future work will focus on incorporating announced disturbances into the model. Besides,
to ensure fair comparisons, the model size was kept comparable to baselines, which may limit the full potential of
the teacher-student framework. Future work will evaluate larger architectures and data augmentation techniques
to enhance performance.
Conclusion
In this work, we developed a Transformer-based deep learning framework for short- to long-term blood glucose
prediction that incorporates knowledge distillation to exploit future disturbance information during training.
The teacher model learns disturbance-aware temporal representations by modeling the influence of anticipated
events, such as meal intake and insulin delivery, on glucose dynamics, while the student model is trained to
approximate these representations using historical data only. This design enables the student to capture future-
relevant features without requiring access to future information at inference time. Experiments conducted on
the OhioT1DM and AZT1D datasets demonstrate that the proposed framework yields consistent improvements
over baseline deep learning models across multiple prediction horizons. The results suggest that knowledge
distillation can serve as a practical mechanism for transferring future-aware representations to deployable
models constrained to historical inputs. From a clinical perspective, the observed improvements are more
pronounced for medium- to long-term forecasts, which may support proactive decision-making such as insulin
adjustment and hypoglycemia risk management. Future work will investigate the incorporation of additional
announced information and the evaluation of the proposed framework in real-time and closed-loop insulin
delivery settings.
